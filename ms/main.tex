\section{Introduction} \label{sec:intro}
Earth is the only planet in the Solar System with liquid water on its surface, and it is the only planet known to host life. The realization that Venus and Mars are hostile to life as we know it is relatively recent in the history of astronomy, which supplanted the belief that life itself was ubiquitous across all planets \citep[see, e.g.,][]{danielson2025universe}. The rocky planets Earth and Venus are approximately the same size, while Mars is slightly smaller, so why is Earth the only planet that sustains oceans and a biosphere?

The region around a star where life can survive has been termed the ``habitable zone'' (HZ). Estimates by \citet{Dole2007HabitableMan} found an optimistic HZ between 0.725--1.24\,AU, due to the use of a simplified representation of a thin atmosphere with fixed surface albedo. Calculations by \citet{hart1979} were the first to consider the increasing luminosity of host star during its main sequence phase, which produced a ``continuously habitable zone'' (CHZ) in which life could avoid a runaway greenhouse at the inner edge and runaway glaciation at the outer edge. \citet{hart1979} found a narrow CHZ range of 0.95--1.01\,AU for the solar system, and a non-existent CHZ for stars later than K0, which would imply that habitable exoplanets may be rare and limited to Sun-like stars. 

Calculations by \citet{kasting1993} suggested that previous models lacked the negative stabilizing feedback on Earth's climate that arises from the carbonate-silicate cycle, in which the long-term abundance of atmospheric CO2 is regulated by temperature-dependent weathering with crustal rocks \citep{walker1981negative}. The \citet{kasting1993} model gave a conservative estimate of the CHZ as 0.95--1.67\,AU, with the inner edge at moist greenhouse limit where water is lost to space and the outer edge at the maximum greenhouse limit for a CO$_2$ atmosphere. \citet{kasting1993} also noted that the CHZ is wider for K- and M-type stars due to their slower main sequence evolution; however, later type stars are also prone to synchronous rotation. Updates to the \citet{kasting1993} model by \citet{kopparapu2013,kopparapu2014} revised the CHZ for the Solar System to 0.99--1.70\,AU, with Earth residing closer to the innermost edge. The key insight from these HZ studies is that tectonically-active terrestrial planets with a means of recycling volatiles (e.g., by a carbonate-silicate cycle) can include self-regulating negative feedback that can maintain habitable conditions despite a brightening host star. If such processes operate on some terrestrial exoplanets, then this implies that habitable exoplanets may be common and may occur around various stellar types.

The value in HZ studies is their operationalization in the search for life. The boundaries of the \citet{kasting1993} and \citet{kopparapu2013,kopparapu2014} HZ provide constraints on the orbital distances at which a tectonically-active planet could maintain surface liquid water around a given star, which can provide a basis for choosing specific targets to observe with large ground- and space-based telescopes. The HZ concept does not categorically rule out the possibility of life existing in other places (for example, on the icy moons of outer planets), and likewise it is also incorrect to presume that a planet within its host stars HZ must inevitably be ``inhabited.'' 

The HZ concept has been widely utilized since its inception and has motivated numerous applications of the concept. A non-comprehensive list of examples includes: 
HZ calculations for dry and land-dominated planets; \citep{abe2011}; hydrogen-dominated greenhouse worlds beyond the classical HZ \citep[e.g.,][]{pierrehumbert2011, ramirez2017}; three-dimensional climate modeling calculations of the inner-edge limits \citep[e.g.,][]{leconte2013,kopparapu2016inner}; occurrence rate calculations for habitable exoplanets \citep[e.g.,][]{kopparapu2013revised,dressing2015,bryson2021occurrence}; characterization of observed exoplanet systems \citep[e.g.,][]{quintana2014, angladaescude2016, gillon2017, robertson2026revised}; and critiques of the HZ concept (\citealt{moore2017, tasker2017, zsom2015}).

The \citet{kasting1993} and \citet{kopparapu2013,kopparapu2014} lineage of models (hereafter referred to as \texttt{Clima}) continue to serve as reference HZ boundaries for guiding observations and for comparing against computationally expensive three-dimensional models. However, no independent validation study of these HZ limits have been conducted, using a comparable radiative-convective equilibrium (RCE) climate model. Although RCE models have a long history in the study of Earth's climate \citep[e.g.,][]{manabe1967}, the \texttt{Clima} lineage has been the only RCE model with the extended capabilities of studying moist atmospheres near the inner edge of the HZ and dense CO$_2$ atmospheres at the outer edge of the HZ.

In this paper we present the \texttt{ExoColumn} RCE model, which was designed to have the same functionality as \texttt{Clima}. \texttt{ExoColumn} includes an independently-developed RCE column model, which is paired with the \texttt{ExoRT} correlated-$k$ radiation package used by the \texttt{ExoCAM} three-dimensional model \citep{wolf2022}. This design choice will enable future hierarchical comparisons between one-dimensional \texttt{ExoColumn} and three-dimensional \texttt{ExoCAM}, with the \texttt{ExoRT} radiative transfer core remaining identical. One-dimensional RCE models also remain valuable tools for exoplanet science, as they can provide more rapid exploration of a large parameter space than is feasible for more computationally expensive models. In the present paper, we present a description of \texttt{ExoColumn}, which is intended as a validation of previous HZ studies with \texttt{Clima} and a demonstration of the model's capabilities for subsequent studies. We first tune the model to present-day Earth conditions and compare the radiative transfer against line-by-line benchmarks (\S\ref{sec:model}). We perform calculations for the inner (\S\ref{sec:inner}) and outer (\S\ref{sec:outer}) edges of the HZ, following the same model configuration as \citep{kopparapu2013,kopparapu2014}. We show HZ limits across spectral types (\S\ref{sec:stellar}) and provide updated HZ boundary coefficients (Table~\ref{tab:hz_coeff}) for community use. 

\section{The ExoColumn model} \label{sec:model}

\texttt{ExoColumn} is a one-dimensional RCE model written in Fortran as standalone software, which depends on \texttt{ExoRT} without any modifications. his gives \texttt{ExoColumn} the flexibility to incorporate any future \texttt{ExoRT} updates. \texttt{ExoColumn} calls the \texttt{ExoRT} radiation routine (\texttt{aerad\_driver}) directly, using the radiative fluxes from \texttt{ExoRT} to calculate equilibrium vertical profiles of temperature. The default configuration uses the n68equiv 68-band scheme and a grid of 70 vertical layers log-spaced from $p_{\rm top}$ to the surface.

The default convection scheme is simplified Betts--Miller \citep{betts1986, bettsmiller1986, frierson2007}, which relaxes the temperature profile toward a moist adiabat and humidity toward a reference relative humidity ($RH_{init}=0.7$). Other convection schemes are also selectable: dry convective adjustment \citep{manabe1964}, Manabe-Wetherald moist adjustment schemes \citep{manabe1967}, and the Zhang--McFarlane scheme \citep{zhang1995}. The water vapor saturation vapor pressure ($q_{\rm sat}$) is calculated with the Clausius-Clapeyron relation. \texttt{ExoColumn} also implements a stratospheric ``cold trap'' intended to represent stratospheric drying due to the Brewer--Dobson cycle. The model defines the cold-point tropopause as the level of minimum saturation mixing ratio, so that an ascending parcel's water vapor is reduced to this value at the tropopause and conserved on its continued ascent into the stratosphere. 

\texttt{ExoColumn} is initialized either from a cold start or from an input profile. In a cold start, the initial temperature is set by integrating a moist adiabat upward from the surface temperature $T_s$, capped at an isothermal stratosphere at $T_{\rm strato}$; water vapor is set to $\mathrm{RH}_{\rm init}\,q_{\rm sat}$ in the troposphere (and dried in the stratosphere). All the dry gases are well-mixed, with an option to include a modern Earth ozone profile \citep{wing2018}. The initial state can also be read from a stored profile. The temperature profile is advanced toward radiative--convective equilibrium by time-marching: at each step \texttt{ExoRT} returns the longwave and shortwave radiative heating rates for the current profile. These are applied to all layer temperatures as well as the surface sensible and latent heat sources in the lowest layer. Convective adjustment then restores the column to a lapse-rate-stable and sub-saturated state. 

The surface is a 10\, slab ocean ($H_{\rm slab}=4.1\times10^{7}\,{\rm J\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}$), with surface temperature calculated with semi-implicit Planck damping. First, the surface upward longwave emission $\sigma T_s^4$ is linearized about the current surface temperature ($T_s$) so that its Planck feedback $4\sigma T_s^3$ is treated implicitly, as:
\begin{equation}
    T_s \leftarrow T_s + \Delta t \frac{F_{net}^{srf}-LE-SH}{H_{slab}+\Delta t\,4\sigma T_s^3},\label{eq:Ts}
\end{equation}
where $F_{net}^{srf}$ is the net surface radiative flux, $LE$ is the latent heat flux, and $SH$ is the sensible heat flux. This is intended to damp temperature overshoots and keep the slab energy budget stable at large time steps The column is marched to equilibrium with an adaptive time step set by a Courant--Friedrichs--Lewy (CFL) stability limit. Each step is sized so that the largest radiative temperature tendency in the column produces a change of at most $\Delta T_{\rm target}=1$ K, with the resulting step restricted to the range $\Delta t\ = [10^{-4},1]$ day. The model is considered converged when either of two criteria is met. \textit{Radiative equilibrium convergence} occurs when the column maximum sum of longwave plus shortwave heating falls below $0.01$ K\,day$^{-1}$ and the net top-of-atmosphere (TOA) flux falls below $0.1$ W\,m$^{-2}$. \textit{Profile stability convergence} occurs when the changes in time-mean profiles over successive 100-day windows fall below fixed thresholds ($\max|\Delta T|<1$ K in the temperature profile, $|\Delta T_s|<0.02$ K at the surface, and $\max|\Delta q|<5\times10^{-4}$ kg\,kg$^{-1}$ in water vapor) while the window-mean TOA flux stays below $0.1$ W\,m$^{-2}$.

\texttt{ExoColumn} has no separate boundary-layer scheme; instead, surface turbulent fluxes use a simplified Monin--Obukhov formulation \citep{frierson2006} that is paired with a surface-coupled mixed layer. The latent and sensible heat fluxes that appear in Eq. (\ref{eq:Ts}) are expressed as
\begin{eqnarray}
SH &=& \rho_a\,c_p\,C\,U\,(\Theta_s - \Theta_a), \label{eq:sh} \\
LE &=& \rho_a\,L(T_s)\,C\,U\,(q^*_s - q_a), \label{eq:le}
\end{eqnarray}
where $\rho_a$ is air density at the lowest model level, $U$ is the near-surface wind speed (a fixed model parameter, default $5\,{\rm m\,s^{-1}}$), $C$ a dimensionless exchange coefficient, $\Theta_s=T_s$ and $\Theta_a=T_a\,(p_0/p_a)^{R_d/c_p}$ are the surface and near-surface potential temperatures, $q^*_s=q_{\rm sat}(T_s)$ is the saturation specific humidity at the surface, $q_a$ the near-surface specific humidity, $c_p$ is the specific heat of dry air, $R_d$ is the gas constant of dry air, and $L(T_s)$ is the latent heat of vaporization evaluated at the surface temperature (which becomes the latent heat of sublimation below the triple point). The value of $C\approx10^{-3}$ is calculated based on a fixed 10\,m reference height in order to be resolution-independent and of Earth-like magnitude. 

\texttt{ExoColumn} includes several optional physics modules (disabled by default) that are needed to make a consistent comparison of the inner and outer edge HZ calculations with \texttt{Clima}. At the inner edge of the HZ, water vapor becomes a major atmospheric constituent and departs from ideal-gas behavior. We implement the non-ideal moist pseudoadiabat described by \citet{kasting1988} and used in \texttt{Clima} as well as the IAPWS-95 non-ideal equation of state for water \citep{wagner2002}, which remains valid up to the critical point (647 K). We also included an option to replace the default MT\_CKD water vapor continuum with the BPS continuum used in \texttt{Clima}. Toward the outer edge of the HZ, dense CO$_2$ atmospheres develop due to reduced weathering rates. We implement an option for CO$_2$ condensation, in which the temperature profile is pinned to the CO$_2$ saturation curve \citep{span1996} wherever the dry adiabat would otherwise supersaturate in CO$_2$ \citep{kasting1991}. This a temperature-dependent CO$_2$ specific heat. We also include an option to perform ``inverse'' climate modeling experiments, in which a temperature profile is specified and the model is run a single step to calculate the corresponding solar flux needed to produce the given surface temperature, which allow us to perform the same set of HZ calculations as \citet{kopparapu2013,kopparapu2014}.

\subsection{Earth validation} \label{sec:earth}
We first validate \texttt{ExoColumn} against present-day Earth. The column is initialized from a cold start with a 1 bar background of N$_2$, O$_2$, and Ar, 400 ppm CO$_2$, and the RCEMIP ozone profile \citep{wing2018}. We calibrate the surface albedo so that the equilibrium surface temperature reaches the observed global-mean value of $T_s\approx288$ K. This use of surface albedo as a ``tuning parameter'' follows the approaches of \citet{kasting1993} and \citet{kopparapu2013,kopparapu2014}, with a physical justification that the tuned albedo accounts for the influence of partial cloud cover that cannot otherwise be represented in a one-dimensional column model. The calibrated surface albedo of 0.2736 allows \texttt{ExoColumn} to reach an equilibrium surface temperature $T_s=287.84$ K, which converges in 2107 steps ($\approx$1000 model days) to a top-of-atmosphere (TOA) flux imbalance below 0.1 W\,m$^{-2}$. The resulting temperature profile, radiative fluxes, heating rates, and water vapor profiles are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:earth_rce}, alongside the \texttt{konrad} RCE model \citep{kluft2019} based on the \texttt{RRMTG} radiative transfer package \citep{mlawer1997}, the \texttt{Clima} model, and the 1976 U.S. Standard Atmosphere for comparison.

The \texttt{ExoColumn} temperature profile matches both \texttt{konrad} and \texttt{Clima} to within a few K through most of the troposphere. All models are consistent with the 1976 U.S. Standard Atmosphere below the tropopause, with significant differences in the stratosphere; this is expected because the U.S. Standard Atmosphere is based on observed averages. All three models share the same qualitative structure with a convecting troposphere at close to the moist adiabatic lapse rate, a temperature minimum near the tropopause, and a stratosphere in radiative equilibrium. The \texttt{ExoColumn} profile exhibits a sharp change in lapse rate (a temperature ``kink'') at the tropopause, where the convectively-adjusted troposphere meets the radiative-equilibrium stratosphere. This abrupt lapse-rate change at the convective top is a property that appears in other RCE models with convective adjustment \citep[see, e.g.,][]{held1982, thuburn2000} and not a numerical artifact. The \texttt{konrad} model produces a smoother tropopause because it applies a hard adjustment scheme to a prescribed moist lapse rate along with a cold-point tropopause coupling intended to produce a present-day Earth profile. The \texttt{Clima} model gives a tropopause at a lower altitude than either \texttt{ExoColumn} or \texttt{konrad}. The three models differ most in the stratosphere, where \texttt{ExoColumn} is the warmest and wettest, and the cold-point tropopause temperature minimum ($\approx$204 K) is about 7 K warmer than \texttt{konrad}. Both \texttt{ExoColumn} and \texttt{konrad} use the same RCEMIP ozone profile, while \texttt{Clima} uses a U.S. Standard Atmosphere ozone profile, which contributes to a cooler stratosphere for \texttt{Clima}. The remaining differences between the models are attributable to differences between the underlying radiative transfer schemes.

\subsection{Radiative-transfer benchmarks} \label{sec:lbl}

We next evaluate the \texttt{ExoRT} and \texttt{Clima} correlated-$k$ radiation cores for regimes relevant to the inner and outer edges of the HZ. We compare these both against independent line-by-line (LBL)
radiative-transfer calculations. The LBL reference is built with a custom code that draws HITRAN line data and Voigt line profiles from the \texttt{RADIS} line-by-line code \citep{pannier2019} and adds a sub-Lorentzian $\chi$-factor \citep{perrin1989} for the CO$_2$ far line wings as well as HITRAN-2024 CO$_2$--CO$_2$ collision-induced absorption. Figure~\ref{fig:lbl} compares the outgoing longwave radiative flux ($F_{\text{IR}}$) of \texttt{ExoRT}, \texttt{Clima}, and the LBL reference for a moist atmosphere near the inner edge of the HZ ($T_s=300$\,K, 1\,bar N$_2$ + 330\,ppm CO$_2$) and a dense-CO$_2$ atmosphere near the outer edge of the HZ ($T_s=273$\,K, 8.87\,ppm CO$_2$ + 1\,bar N$_2$). 

\begin{figure*}[ht!]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{reference/max_greenhouse/lbl_olr_benchmark_2panel.pdf}
\caption{Comparison of the \texttt{ExoRT} (red) and \texttt{Clima} (green) correlated-$k$ radiation cores against a line-by-line (LBL) reference (grey, black) for an atmospheric column near the inner edge (left) and outer edge (right) of the habitable zone.}
\label{fig:lbl}
\end{figure*}

Both \texttt{ExoRT} and \texttt{Clima} agree with the LBL reference to within 1\%. The differences are almost entirely due to the different generations of spectroscopic line data used in each model. The LBL reference itself is based on the HITRAN-2020 line list \citep{gordon2022}, although differences from HITRAN-2016 in the bands that dominate the outgoing longwave radiative flux are less than 1\%. The \texttt{ExoRT} n68equiv $k$-coefficients are computed from the HITRAN-2016 line lists \citep{gordon2017} for H$_2$O, CO$_2$, and CH$_4$. The \texttt{Clima} $k$-coefficients of \citet{kopparapu2013} instead use the HITRAN-2008 line lists \citep{rothman2009}, supplemented by the high-temperature HITEMP-2010 database \citep{rothman2010} for near-infrared H$_2$O bands that dominate the absorbed stellar flux near the inner edge. Note that a more recent version of \texttt{Clima} \citep{vidaurri2022outer} has implemented new $k$-coefficients from HITRAN-2016 that remove most of the differences with \texttt{ExoRT} (not shown). We show the 2013-era \texttt{Clima} results in this paper to remain consistent with the HZ results reported by \citet{kopparapu2013,kopparapu2014}. Differences between \texttt{ExoColumn} and \texttt{Clima} occur in the pure-rotational H$_2$O band in the far infrared ($\lesssim600$ cm$^{-1}$, $\gtrsim17\,\mu$m; $+8.6$ W\,m$^{-2}$) and across the 750--1250 cm$^{-1}$ window (8--13 $\mu$m; $+5.6$ W\,m$^{-2}$). These differences in the underlying radiative transfer core are the primary source of differences between the  \texttt{Clima} HZ calculations  by \citet{kopparapu2013,kopparapu2014} and those described next with \texttt{ExoColumn}.


\section{The inner edge of the habitable zone} \label{sec:inner}

We calculate the inner edge of the HZ for Earth following the inverse climate modeling method of \citet{kasting1988} and \citet{kopparapu2013}. Instead of time-marching each case to equilibrium, we prescribe a surface temperature $T_s$ and build a vertical profile for a fully water-saturated column. We integrate upward from $T_s$ with a non-ideal moist pseudoadiabat \citet{kasting1988} and the IAPWS-95 steam equation of state \citep{wagner2002}, with an isothermal stratosphere of $T_{\rm strato}=200$ K. The \texttt{ExoRT} radiation core is then called a single step to return the net outgoing infrared radiative flux, $F_{\text{IR}}$, and the net incident solar radiative flux, $F_{\text{SOL}}$, for the prescribed column. (Note that $F_{\text{SOL}}$ is computed with a six-point Gauss--Legendre hemispheric average over the solar zenith angle, following \citet{kopparapu2013}.) The effective stellar flux that would sustain each column can then be calculated as $S_{\rm eff}=F_{\text{IR}}/F_{\text{SOL}}$, so that $S_{\rm eff}>1$ denotes a planet receiving greater insolation than present-day Earth. These calculations assume a background 1\,bar atmosphere with 78\% N$_2$, 21\% O$_2$, 1\% Ar, and 330 ppm CO$_2$, prognostic H$_2$O, and no O$_3$ or CH$_4$. We assume a fixed surface albedo of 0.32 to match the value used by \citet{kopparapu2013} (using a different albedo value for fixed-profile inverse calculations would result in a different energy balance). We perform the set of calculations by increasing $T_s$ upward from present-day values to the point where the column transitions into a moist and then runaway greenhouse state.

The results of these \texttt{ExoColumn} calculations for the inner edge of the HZ are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:hz_inner}, following the same format as \citet[][Fig. 3]{kopparapu2013}. Values from \texttt{Clima} are also shown as well as a version of \texttt{ExoColumn} using the BPS water vapor continuum (used by \texttt{Clima}) instead of the default MT\_CKD continuum. Figure~\ref{fig:hz_inner} (top left) shows that $F_{\text{IR}}$ and $F_{\text{SOL}}$ steadily increase as the surface warms and water vapor comes to dominate the atmosphere, with $F_{\text{IR}}$ peaking near the Simpson--Nakajima radiation limit \citep{nakajima1992} of $\approx$295 W\,m$^{-2}$ (1.3\% above the \citeauthor{kopparapu2013} value of 291 W\,m$^{-2}$) as most of the atmosphere becomes opaque to infrared radiation. $F_{\text{SOL}}$ likewise increases due to increased near-infrared absorption but then reaches a plateau as Rayleigh scattering increases. Further increases in $F_{\text{IR}}$ occur near 2000\,K as the surface begins to radiate at visible and near-infrared wavelengths. This behavior is shown in the changes of planetary albedo (calculated as the ratio of the upward and downward solar fluxes) with surface temperature (Figure~\ref{fig:hz_inner}, top right). The minimum value of planetary albedo corresponds to the maximum value in $F_{\text{IR}}$, which both then flatten out at higher surface temperatures. 

\begin{figure*}[ht!]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{reference/moist_runaway/hz_inner_nonideal.pdf}
\caption{Inner edge of the HZ for a Sun-like star. Calculations with \texttt{ExoColumn} are shown using the the default MT\_CKD continuum (solid) and the BPS continuum (dotted), alongside \texttt{Clima} calculations with the BPS continuum for comparison (dashed). Panels show (top left) net outgoing infrared flux and net incident solar flux, (top right) planetary albedo, (bottom left) effective solar flux, and (bottom right) sample non-ideal pseudoadiabatic water vapor profiles. \label{fig:hz_inner}

The first inner HZ limit is the moist greenhouse limit, which is is the point at which the stratospheric H$_2$O mixing ratio reaches $3\times10^{-3}$, above which water is efficiently photodissociated and hydrogen escapes to space \citep{kasting1988, kopparapu2013}. This change in water vapor content occurs between $T_s$ = 320\,K and 340\,K, with over an order of magnitude increase in stratospheric water vapor (Figure~\ref{fig:hz_inner}, bottom right). This moist greenhouse limit occurs in the \texttt{ExoColumn} results at $S_{\rm eff} = 1.087$ (Figure~\ref{fig:hz_inner}, bottom left), which corresponds to an orbital distance of $d=1/\sqrt{S_{\rm eff}}=0.959$\,AU. For comparison, the \texttt{ExoColumn} BPS-continuum moist greenhouse limit is at $S_{\rm eff} = 1.074$ (0.965\,AU), and the \texttt{Clima} limit is $S_{\rm eff} = 1.016$ (0.992\,AU).

The second inner HZ limit is the runaway greenhouse limit at which the oceans completely evaporate. The runaway greenhouse limit occurs in the \texttt{ExoColumn} results at $S_{\rm eff} = 1.101$ (Figure~\ref{fig:hz_inner}, bottom left), which corresponds to an orbital distance of $d=1/\sqrt{S_{\rm eff}}=0.953$\,AU. This is the value of $S_{\rm eff}$ where the model first reaches the Simpson--Nakajima radiation limit \citep{nakajima1992}. For comparison, the \texttt{ExoColumn} BPS-continuum runaway greenhouse limit is at $S_{\rm eff} = 1.093$ (0.956\,AU), and the \texttt{Clima} limit is $S_{\rm eff} = 1.060$ (0.971\,AU). 

The offsets between the \texttt{ExoColumn} and \texttt{Clima} model are modest and almost entirely due to the different correlated-$k$ radiation cores used by each model (see \S\ref{sec:lbl}). Our experiments included an implementation of the BMS water vapor continuum to more directly compare with \texttt{Clima}, but this only accounts for a fraction of the differences between the model results. Nevertheless, these \texttt{ExoColumn} results give moist and runaway greenhouse limits with HZ distances that agree with those published by \citet{kopparapu2013} within $\sim$2\%.


\section{The outer edge of the habitable zone} \label{sec:outer}

We calculate the outer edge of the HZ as the maximum greenhouse limit for a CO$_2$-dominated atmosphere, following the inverse modeling method of \citet{kasting1993} and \citet{kopparapu2013}. Here the surface
temperature is held fixed at 273 K over a 1 bar N$_2$ background, and the CO$_2$ partial pressure is swept from 1 bar up to 34.7 bar. For each CO$_2$ partial pressure, we build a water-saturated moist adiabat in which the temperature profile is pinned to the CO$_2$ saturation curve wherever the dry adiabat would otherwise supersaturate (i.e., CO$_2$ condensation; \citealt{kasting1991}, with the \citealt{span1996} saturation curve). The CO$_2$ contribution to the specific heat is evaluated at the local temperature, and the stratosphere is isothermal at 154 K. As in the inner-edge calculation, the \texttt{ExoRT} radiative core is then called a single step to return $F_{\text{IR}}$ and $F_{\text{SOL}}$, with the ratio giving $S_{\rm eff}$. The maximum-greenhouse limit, denoting the outer edge of the HZ, is the minimum value of $S_{\rm eff}$ over the range of CO$_2$ partial pressures.

The results of the \texttt{ExoColumn} calculations for the outer edge of the HZ are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:hz_outer}, following the same three-panel format as \citet[][Fig. 5]{kopparapu2013}. Values from \texttt{Clima} are also shown for comparison. Figure~\ref{fig:hz_outer} (top) shows that $F_{\rm IR}$ and $F_{\rm SOL}$ decrease as the CO$_2$ partial pressure increases, which indicates the increasing greenhouse effect of CO$_2$. Beyond $\sim$10 bar, $F_{\rm IR}$ reaches a floor of $\approx$72 W\,m$^{-2}$ as the thick CO$_2$ atmosphere becomes opaque at infrared wavelengths, while $F_{\rm SOL}$ continues to decrease as Rayleigh scattering increases. This corresponds to a high planetary albedo as CO$_2$ partial pressure increases (Figure~\ref{fig:hz_outer}, middle). The competing tendencies of $F_{\rm IR}$ and $F_{\rm SOL}$ lead to a minimum $S_{\rm eff}=0.395$ ($1.59$ AU) at a CO$_2$ partial pressure of 8.9 bar. This is the maximum greenhouse limit, beyond which $S_{\rm eff}$ decreases as the cooling by Rayleigh scattering dominates any increased greenhouse warming from additional CO$_2$.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{reference/max_greenhouse/hz_outer.pdf}
\caption{Outer edge of the HZ for a Sun-like star. Calculations with \texttt{ExoColumn} (solid) are shown alongside values from \texttt{Clima} (dashed). Panels show  (top) net outgoing infrared flux and net incident solar flux, (middle) planetary albedo, and (bottom) effective solar flux. The maximum greenhouse limit marked on the bottom panel is $S_{\rm eff}=0.395$ ($1.59$ AU) at a CO$_2$ partial pressure of 8.9 bar. The \texttt{Clima} maximum greenhouse limit calculated by \citet{kopparapu2013} is $S_{\rm eff}= 0.343$ (1.70 AU) at a CO$_2$ partial pressure of $\sim$8 bar. The \texttt{ExoColumn} limit is thus more conservative, which places the outer edge closer to the star by $\sim$6\%. This difference is driven by the dense-CO$_2$ offsets in the two correlated-$k$ radiation cores discussed in Section~\ref{sec:lbl}. We also note that the \texttt{ExoColumn} calculations above 10 bar are performed by clamping the correlated-$k$ lookup at the top of the \texttt{ExoRT} pressure table. This freezes the pressure broadening at its 10 bar value, while the collision-induced absorption and continuum opacities continue to use the true pressure.


\section{Habitable-zone limits across stellar spectral types} \label{sec:stellar}

We now proceed to calculate the boundaries of the HZ for main sequence stars, following the method first performed by \citet{kasting1993} and then by \citet{kopparapu2013} using versions of \texttt{Clima}. We compute the HZ limits with \texttt{ExoColumn} for nine host stars spanning spectral types F (7200 K) through G (5780 K, the Sun), K (4000--4800 K), and M (2600--3700 K), plus cooler stars down to 2000\,K, using BT-Settl spectral energy distributions (SEDs) \citep{allard2012}. 

Calculations of the inner and outer HZ edge for all nine host stars is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:hz_fig6}, following the four-panel format as \citet[][Fig. 6]{kopparapu2013}. Planetary albedo (top left for inner edge, bottom left for outer edge) is higher for F-dwarf host stars and lowest for M-dwarf host stars, which occurs for two reasons. First is that the stellar spectral energy distribution of F-type stars is shifted toward shorter wavelengths, which increases the Rayleigh scattering of incident light. Second is that water vapor and CO$_2$ absorb more strongly at near-infrared than visible wavelengths, which results in M-dwarf plants absorbing a larger fraction of incident light. These effects are most pronounced for dense atmospheres, such as water-vapor rich atmospheres near the inner edge or dense CO$_2$ atmospheres near the outer edge.

\begin{figure*}[ht!]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{reference/habitablezone/hz_figure6.pdf}
\caption{Habitable zone limits for nine host stars ranging from M (2600\,K) to F (7200\,K). Inner edge calculations are shown on the top row and outer edge calculations are shown on the bottom row.}
\label{fig:hz_fig6}
\end{figure*}

These HZ boundaries are summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:hz_fig7}, following the two-panel format of \citet[][Fig. 7]{kopparapu2013}. The top panel shows $S_{\rm eff}$ at the moist- and maximum-greenhouse limits as a function of stellar effective temperature, and the bottom panel converts these limits to orbital distance as a function of stellar mass, using the \citet{baraffe1998} 5\,Gyr solar-metallicity relationships for stellar mass, luminosity, and effective temperature. The dashed curves in Figure~\ref{fig:hz_fig7} show the corresponding \textbf{Clima} boundaries \citep{kopparapu2013}. The \texttt{ExoColumn} inner and outer limits both shift inward toward the star across all stellar types. The primary driver of this shift is the different correlated-$k$ radiation cores used in the two models (see \S\ref{sec:lbl}). The net width of the HZ remains approximately the same as \texttt{Clima}, and the differences between the two models are only of a few percent when shown as a function of orbital distance (Figure~\ref{fig:hz_fig7}, bottom panel). These results provide encouraging validation that the previously-published \texttt{Clima} calculations of the HZ are robust when assessed with an independent model.

We fit the resulting HZ boundaries, following the quartic form of \citet{kopparapu2013},
\begin{equation}
    S_{\rm eff}(T_{\rm eff})=S_{\rm eff,\odot}+a\,T_\star+b\,T_\star^2+c\,T_\star^3+d\,T_\star^4,\label{eq:fit}
\end{equation}
with $T_\star=T_{\rm eff}-5780$\,K. This allows the associated HZ distances to be calculated as
\begin{equation}
    d=\left(\frac{L/L_{\odot}}{S_{\rm eff}}\right)^{0.5}\,\text{AU},\label{eq:distance}
\end{equation}
where $L/L_{\odot}$ is the relative luminosity of the star to the sun. This expression fits the \texttt{ExoColumn} model results with a root-mean-square residual of $\sim10^{-3}\,S_\odot$ over 2000--7200\,K. The resulting coefficients are listed in Table~\ref{tab:hz_coeff}, with $S_{\rm eff,\odot}=1.10$, 1.09, and 0.39 for the runaway-, moist-, and maximum-greenhouse limits, respectively. This table can be directly compared with the corresponding \texttt{Clima} polynomial \citep[][Tab. 3]{kopparapu2013}.

Finally, we examine the dependence of these limits on planetary mass by calculating the HZ boundaries for planets of 0.1, 1, and 5 $M_\oplus$ (Figure~\ref{fig:hz_mass}), We follow the approach of \citet{kopparapu2014}, which uses the mass--radius relation $M_p/M_\oplus=0.968\,(R_p/R_\oplus)^{3.2}$ for $M_p<5\,M_\oplus$ and assumes that the background N$_2$ pressure scales with planetary radius as
$P_s/P_\oplus=0.937\,(R_p/R_\oplus)^{2.40}$. These relationships can be expressed using powers of mass, as $g\propto M^{1-2/3.2}=M^{0.375}$ and $p_{\rm N_2}\propto M^{2.40/3.2}=M^{0.75}$. We renormalize both $g$ and $p_{\rm N_2}$ so that 1\,$M_\oplus$ gives exactly Earth values, with the renormalizing applied at all masses. (Without renormalizing, 1\,$M_\oplus$  would give $g=9.61$\,m\,s$^{-2}$ and $p_{\rm N_2}=$0.96\,bar.) The three planetary masses considered correspond to surface gravities of 4.14, 9.81, and 17.93\,m\,s$^{-2}$ and N$_2$ inventories of 0.18, 1.0, and 3.34\,bar. A more massive planet has both a higher surface gravity and a larger N$_2$ column, which reduces the water vapor column depth and shifts the inner edge of the HZ closer to the host star. The outer edge remains relatively insensitive to changes in mass. 

The dashed curves in Figure~\ref{fig:hz_mass} show the comparable \texttt{Clima} boundaries for the dependence of the HZ on planetary mass \citet[][Fig. 3]{kopparapu2014}. The 1\,$M_\oplus$ cases show a close match for the Sun, with $S_{\rm eff} = 1.102$ for \texttt{ExoColumn} and 1.107 for \texttt{Clima}. The mass ordering is likewise consistent between the models, with higher gravity yielding a smaller atmospheric column that shifts the HZ inward. However, the solutions with \texttt{ExoColumn} at different planetary masses occupy a somewhat compressed range compared to \texttt{Clima}. This again is primarily due to the differences in the correlated-$k$ radiative transfer cores (\S\ref{sec:lbl}).

In summary, \texttt{ExoColumn} is capable of reproducing all HZ calculations that have been performed with \texttt{Clima}. The differences in the model results can be understood as a result of differences in radiative transfer, and even with these differences the results differ by a margin of a few percent.

\section{Discussion \& Conclusion} \label{sec:discussion}

We have presented \texttt{ExoColumn} as a new one-dimensional RCE model with all the physical capabilities needed to perform HZ calculations comparable to those with \texttt{Clima}. The value in constructing this new model is that it leverages the existing \texttt{ExoRT} radiative transfer core, also used in the \texttt{ExoCAM} three-dimensional model, which will enable self-consistent comparisons between these two models with different dimensionality but the exact same radiative transfer. \texttt{ExoColumn} is also freely available for community use and will be included in ongoing exoplanetary model intercomparison projects \citep{sohl2024cuisines}.

These calculations also provide an independent validation of the \texttt{Clima} HZ boundaries \citep{kasting1993,kopparapu2013,kopparapu2014}. The \texttt{Clima} has remained an influential reference in astrobiology for over three decades, and continues to be used today. During this time, three-dimensional models have also provided constraints on HZ limits, with the most significant contributions in the M-dwarf regime where synchronous rotation can significantly change the results obtained by one-dimensional models \citep[see, e.g.,][for a review]{shields2016habitability}. However, it remains challenging for any single three-dimensional model to comprehensively explore the inner and outer HZ boundaries for a range of stellar types as was done with \texttt{Clima}. This study with \texttt{ExoColumn} represents the first comprehensive attempt at validating the \texttt{Clima} results using a model of a different lineage. The results shown in this paper are a testament to the robustness of \texttt{Clima} in its original \citep{kasting1993} and updated \citep{kopparapu2013,kopparapu2014} forms.
